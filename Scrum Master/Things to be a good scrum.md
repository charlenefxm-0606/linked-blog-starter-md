The mission is the meeting’s ongoing purpose; the goal is what participants should leave with.

|Meeting|Cadence|Mission and expected result|Simple agenda|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Sprint Planning**|Beginning of every Sprint|Agree on a valuable Sprint Goal and a realistic delivery plan.|Review priorities and availability → agree on the goal → Developers select work → plan how to complete it.|
|**Daily Scrum**|Every working day, **15 minutes**|Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adjust the next day’s work.|What needs to move forward? → What is blocked? → Who needs to coordinate or help?|
|**Backlog refinement**|Start with **one session weekly**, adjusting to need|Prepare upcoming work so it is clear enough to consider during planning.|Clarify items and acceptance criteria → identify dependencies → split large items → Developers estimate.|
|**Sprint Review**|Near the end of every Sprint, before the retrospective|Inspect product results with stakeholders and discuss what to do next.|Review progress toward goals → inspect completed results → gather feedback → discuss adaptations.|
|**Sprint Retrospective**|At the end of every Sprint, after Review|Identify practical ways to improve quality and effectiveness.|What helped? → What slowed us down? → Choose an improvement → agree how to try it.|
|**Sprint-close and reporting check**|Near Sprint end, coordinated with Matt|Ensure records and reports accurately reflect what happened.|Check completion and unfinished work → verify required fields and release links → capture metrics → close records through the agreed process.|

The Sprint-close check is a **local administrative activity**, not a formal Scrum event. Refinement is an ongoing activity; its scheduled session is also a team choice.

For a two-week Sprint, your class’s suggested allocations—up to four hours for Planning, two hours for Review, and 90 minutes for Retro—are reasonable starting points. Scrum does not require exact proportional timeboxes for shorter Sprints.

**Recommended Sprint metrics**

Start with a small report that answers useful questions.

|Metric|What it tells you|How to use it|
|---|---|---|
|**Sprint Goal achieved, with explanation**|Whether the team accomplished its intended result|Lead the report with the outcome.|
|**Planned versus completed work**|How realistic the starting plan was|Record the starting scope, completed work, and changes during the Sprint.|
|**Velocity across recent comparable Sprints**|How much work this team usually finishes|Support future forecasts using consistent estimation and completion rules.|
|**Carryover and its causes**|Why work moves into another Sprint|Look for dependencies, oversized stories, testing delays, or interruptions.|
|**Blocked work and time blocked**|Where progress needs help|Identify the impact, the person who can help, and the next action.|
|**Available capacity**|Who is actually available|Account for PTO, onboarding, departures, and other responsibilities.|
|**VersionOne compliance issues**|Whether records meet local requirements|Catch missing information before closure and reduce recurring errors.|

For QA, consider one additional quality measure once Fei and Matt agree on what it should inform—for example, unresolved defects by severity.

**Keep points and hours separate.** Hours describe time; story points express relative size. Neither should automatically become an individual performance score.

A simple Sprint report could use this format:

> **Sprint Goal:** Achieved / not achieved, with explanation.  
> **Delivery:** Planned X points; completed Y; added or removed Z during the Sprint.  
> **Capacity:** Relevant absences and staffing changes.  
> **Blockers and risks:** Impact, help needed, and next action.  
> **Quality:** Relevant testing or defect concerns.  
> **Recordkeeping:** Outstanding VersionOne corrections.  
> **Improvement:** What the team will try next Sprint.

**How to be successful on this team—in everyday language**

1. **Make expectations clear.**

2. **Help people understand the purpose of the Sprint.**

3. **Make meetings worth attending.**  
    Start with a purpose, keep discussion relevant, and finish with clear next steps. Move detailed troubleshooting into a separate conversation with the right people.
    
4. **Notice when work gets stuck and follow through.**  
    Track the obstacle, its impact, who can help, and the next follow-up. Bring Matt or Fei a specific request when their help is needed.
    
5. **Help the team make realistic plans.**  
    Account for availability, onboarding, dependencies, and previous experience. Include the time you need for Scrum Master work.
    
6. **Help people keep their own records accurate.**  
    Explain the required information and check it before closure. Build a habit the team can maintain.
    
7. **Use numbers to ask better questions.**  
    If planned and completed work differ, ask what changed. Look for patterns before drawing conclusions about performance.
    
8. **Protect the meaning of Done.**  
    Confirm the team’s quality standards, including testing evidence, defect handling, and any required integration.
    
9. **Create room for people to speak honestly.**  
    Treat requests for help and early warnings as useful information. Understand concerns before deciding on a response.
    
10. **Follow through on one improvement each Sprint.**  
    Choose something specific, try it, and check whether it helped. For example: confirm test-data availability before selecting dependent work.
